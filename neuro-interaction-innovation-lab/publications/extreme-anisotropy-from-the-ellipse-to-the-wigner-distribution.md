# Extreme anisotropy: from the ellipse to the Wigner distribution

## Abstract

<jats:title>Abstract</jats:title>
                  <jats:p>The Dirac delta function and the Wigner semicircle distribution are central pillars of distribution theory and the physics of complex systems (random matrices), respectively. Traditionally, these concepts are taught in separate compartments — the former via sequences of one-dimensional functions, the latter via advanced methods of probabilistic linear algebra. In this paper, we propose a geometric bridge between these two worlds starting from an elementary object: the ellipse. We show that the limit of ``extreme anisotropy'' of a uniform ellipse — infinite stretching in one direction and collapse in the orthogonal direction — when normalized by the scaling law \(h_a = a/2\), converges in the sense of distributions to the line delta \(\delta(y)\). The longitudinal projection of this ellipse before the limit reveals the density \(\sqrt{1 - u^2}\), which is functionally identical to the Wigner semicircle profile after appropriate rescaling and normalization. We emphasize that this connection is formal and geometric, not causal: the construction does not derive the Wigner semicircle law from random matrix theory, but rather provides a visual, elementary analogue of it. The method uses only elementary calculus (Mean Value Theorem, Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem) and analytic geometry, making it accessible to undergraduate students in physics and mathematics. The paper offers four contributions: (i) an explicit, elementary construction of the line delta from area-preserving anisotropic ellipses; (ii) a general scaling theorem demonstrating the universality of this collapse for a broad class of compact domains; (iii) a topological distinction between compact quadrics, which converge to the line delta, and non-compact quadrics, which escape to infinity and converge to zero; and (iv) a pedagogical bridge connecting elementary geometry to the functional form of the Wigner distribution. We discuss limitations of the formalism for non-symmetric tensors (Hall effect) and for other conics (hyperbolas, parabolas), pointing to directions for future work.</jats:p>
