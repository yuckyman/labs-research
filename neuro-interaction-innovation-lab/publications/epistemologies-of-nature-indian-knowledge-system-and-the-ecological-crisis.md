# Epistemologies of Nature: Indian Knowledge System and the Ecological Crisis

## Abstract

<jats:p>The current ecological crisis – marked by climate change, biodiversity loss, and environmental degradation – calls for a re-examination of diverse epistemological traditions. This paper explores Indian Knowledge Systems (IKS) as a repository of ecological wisdom that provides alternative frameworks for addressing environmental challenges. While dominant Western paradigms often stress mechanistic and extractive approaches, IKS offers a holistic, relational, and ethical perspective. The study examines the epistemological bases of Indian traditions and their relevance to contemporary ecological discourse. It highlights the epistemic marginalization of indigenous and non-Western knowledge in environmental governance and policy-making. Given the urgent need for sustainable and equitable responses, the research asks: How do Indian traditions frame human–nature relationships? What ecological ethics emerge from their epistemologies? How did colonialism disrupt indigenous knowledge? And can these frameworks inform ecological restoration today? Engaging with Vedic, Buddhist, and Jain traditions, the study uses textual analysis, historical inquiry, and discourse analysis. Drawing on decolonial thought, environmental philosophy, and epistemology, it develops a framework that situates IKS within modern ecological debates. Findings reveal that Indian epistemologies emphasize interconnectedness, non-anthropocentric ethics, and cyclical understandings of time and nature – contrasting sharply with industrial models grounded in linear, exploitative use of resources. The ecological interpretation of dharma foregrounds duties towards nature rather than rights over it. The research ultimately calls for epistemic justice in environmental governance, while offering community-based, tradition-informed pathways to sustainability, resilience, and ecological restoration.</jats:p>
