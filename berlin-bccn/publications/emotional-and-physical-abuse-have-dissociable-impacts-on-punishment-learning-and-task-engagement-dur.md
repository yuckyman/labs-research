# Emotional and physical abuse have dissociable impacts on punishment learning and task engagement during adolescence

## Abstract

<p>Childhood adversity is a robust risk factor for psychopathology, and growing evidence implicates developmental alterations in reward and punishment learning as potent mechanisms shaping this vulnerability. However, little is known about how specific types of adversity impact different learning processes, particularly during the sensitive adolescent period. To examine effects of different types of adversity on the development of valenced learning processes, we combined a subtype-specific approach to childhood adversity, a behavioral task and reinforcement learning (RL) model that capture distinct component processes of learning, and a longitudinal design in youth aged 9-17 (n = 166). Task performance metrics included traditional summary scores and RL-derived estimates for reward learning rate, punishment learning rate, win/loss frequency sensitivity, and go bias (general tendency to play). Analyses probing adversity subtypes and component learning processes revealed dissociable effects:  emotional abuse attenuated normative developmental increases in punishment learning rate, and physical abuse attenuated normative increases in both go bias and a summary score of reward learning (FDR &amp;lt; .05). In contrast, analyses relying on a gross summary score of net success and composite variables representing broad adversity dimensions (abuse vs. neglect) obscured these effects, highlighting the need for fine-grained characterization of both adversity and learning processes. Additionally, adversity did not predict differences in net success, suggesting that youth high and low in adversity reach similar behavioral outcomes through divergent learning strategies. This study is among the first to identify unique effects of different adversity types on specific component processes of valenced learning during adolescence.</p>
