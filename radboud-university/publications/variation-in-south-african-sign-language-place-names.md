# Variation in South African Sign Language Place Names

## Abstract

<jats:p>&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;OVERVIEW&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;This dataset accompanies the ‘Variation in SASL Place Names’ archive on Omeka S: https://pokello.ufs.ac.za/s/sasl/page/welcome. It presents a selection of place-name signs collected during a research project. Place names are fascinating linguistic and cultural entities in themselves. However, the high level of variation in SASL is reflected in the range of name signs associated with single places. This dataset demonstrates a sample of SASL place-name variants as well as the factors that form them. This is not a definitive list of place-name signs, nor should it be used in any way as an official terminology list. Instead, it allows an exploration of variation from a scholarly and curiosity perspective.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;PURPOSE/USE&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;The aim of the research was to initiate the systematic collection and documentation of SASL place names. Additionally, an element of sociolinguistic analysis was included in order to contextualise the variation found. Where available, etymological information is provided in the description of each sign.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;Place names in signed languages are not simply adaptations of those from spoken languages. Instead, place-name signs are allocated by Deaf communities according to their experience, in the same way that different hearing sociolinguistic communities assign names in line with their experience. Deaf communities tend to form geographic clusters, particularly in places where there are schools for the Deaf. There are different signing traditions (i.e., ways of creating signs) as well as ways to teach and learn sign language. As a result, various Deaf communities tend to create their own place-name signs for places that are relevant to their experience, and these may or may not be the same as signs allocated by other Deaf communities. Instead of standardisation, a process of conventionalisation is followed, whereby the community agrees on which place-name signs to use. This community could be the local one, but also extend to national level. The result is a dynamic toponymy that exists in parallel to the spoken one.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;This dataset allows interested parties to explore geographical, generational, and diachronic variation in SASL through place names.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;DATA COLLECTION&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;Primary data: Video recordings of semi-structured individual and group interviews with members of the South African Deaf community (in the Free State and Northern Cape provinces) were annotated and translated in ELAN. The questionnaires included questions to elicited responses about sociolinguistic variables, such as age (generational variation), where the participant spent most of their life (geographic variation), and what spoken language was used in their home (language contact). Place-name signs were isolated and rerecorded by Deaf signers. Details of the variants (place-name signs) such as the sociolinguistic variables, date recorded, and research site, were recorded in an Excel spreadsheet.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;Secondary data: Video recordings of place-name signs collected by the National Institute for the Deaf (in the Western Cape province) for their SASL Video Dictionary were shared as per MoA. The signs were rerecorded by Deaf signers for inclusion in this dataset. No supplementary background was provided.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;Etymological information: At this stage the information is mostly anecdotal, as signed toponymy is a novice research field. To keep interviews to a reasonable length, the questionnaires did not include an etymological section. In some cases, participants did provide more information on specific signs. However, most of the information is provided by the research team (Department of South African Sign Language and Deaf Studies, UFS) and knowledge they have acquired through years-long interaction with the Deaf community.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;DISCLAIMER&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;The selection of SASL place names is a reflection of variation in SASL, and should not be considered an official terminology list. This collection of data serves as a way to explore the interesting background to place names, as well as the variation in SASL. In this research, new information is continuously uncovered. Users, especially those conducting research, are encouraged to consult original resources and to approach the material with critical consideration. For formally verified place-name signs, please contact the Pan South African Language Board (PanSALB) or see the Dictionary of the National Institute for the Deaf (NID).&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;ETHICS STATEMENT&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;Primary data: Ethical clearance was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Council of the University of the Free State: UFS-HSD2023/1614/4. Subject to the conditions of this clearance, informed consent was obtained from all participants, including the use of their data.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;Secondary data: The place-name signs provided by the NID were re-recorded in accordance with the MoA.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;Signers: The signers of the re-recorded place-name signs signed consent forms on the use of their image.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;This research is the product of a project, Advancing SASL for 4IR Technological Development using Place Names, funded by the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture (South Africa), April 2022 – June 2025. It is a collaborative project between the Department of South African Sign Language and Deaf Studies and the Interdisciplinary Centre for Digital Futures, both at the University of the Free State (South Africa).&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;The following people made notable contributions to the sociolinguistic component of the project: Dr Chrismi Loth, Dr Patrick Sibanda, Dr Sara Siyavoshi, Jani de Lange, Prof Annalene van Staden, Emily Matabane, Susan Lombaard, Prof Theodorus du Plessis, Donovan Wright, Lucia Mamotete Mapeshoane, Kirsten de Villiers, Gloria Motshoeneng, Nhlanhla Simelane, Anele Kotoyi and Annemarie le Roux.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;The following people made notable contributions to the computational component of the project: Dr Herkulaas Combrink, Charne Bornman, Nkateko Nkuna, Priscilla Keche, Taylon Colbert, Aviwe Matoti, Jarryd Trip, Valusha Oelofse and Molemo Dibe.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;The National Institute for the Deaf (South Africa) is acknowledged for their support and their contribution of place-name signs.&lt;/p&gt;&lt;p dir="ltr"&gt;We would like to express our gratitude to the participating Deaf communities for welcoming us into their spaces, for generously sharing their knowledge with us, and for graciously providing solutions and suggestions to improve our protocols.&lt;/p&gt;</jats:p>
