# Genome and piliome analysis of E. coli isolates from human colorectal cancer biopsies

## Abstract

<title>Abstract</title>
                <p>
                  Colorectal cancer (CRC) has been linked to specific members of the gut microbiota, including adherent-invasive
                  <italic>Escherichia coli</italic>
                  (AIEC), yet the strain-level features that underpin pathogenicity and clinical relevance remain incompletely defined. Here, we isolated
                  <italic>E. coli</italic>
                  directly from tumour and adjacent healthy colorectal biopsies of six CRC patients and performed whole-genome sequencing and functional phenotyping. Nine genetically distinct
                  <italic>E. coli</italic>
                  strains were identified, spanning phylogroups A and B2 and multiple pandemic sequence types, revealing substantial intra- and inter-patient heterogeneity. Only a subset of isolates displayed AIEC-like adhesion and invasion phenotypes, underscoring the phenotypic plasticity of CRC-associated
                  <italic>E. coli</italic>
                  . One isolate with preferential recovery from tumour tissue exhibited pronounced cytotoxicity and harboured strain-specific oncogenic risk factors, including the
                  <italic>pks</italic>
                  colibactin genomic island and a high-virulence allele of the type 1 pilus adhesin FimH. In depth analysis of
                  <italic>fimH</italic>
                  revealed intra-patient allelic variation, indicating that single representative isolates may not fully capture virulence potential within individual hosts. More broadly, comparative genomic analyses showed that chaperone-usher pilus repertoires and previously proposed AIEC and CRC biomarkers (
                  <italic>chuA, eefC, fitA</italic>
                  ) were primarily associated with phylogroup rather than tumor-tropic colonization. Together, these findings demonstrate that CRC-associated
                  <italic>E. coli</italic>
                  constitute a diverse population lacking uniform genetic signatures. Our results highlight the importance of strain-resolved genomic and functional profiling to better understand bacterial contributions to colorectal cancer.
                </p>
